FocusFinder 2026

A desk robot that notices when you stop paying attention. Animated eyes on a transparent screen follow you while a camera watches your eyes, and when you drift off a light flashes red and a buzzer reminds you to get back to work.

A team project for Cornerstone of Engineering II at NU London, with Juan Andres Escotet, Ming-Wei Hu and Filip Tomic. I led the team, designed the enclosure in SolidWorks, set up the Pi, camera and focus-detection software, and did the wiring and assembly.

Why a desk robot

App blockers and Pomodoro timers only work if you remember to use them. We wanted something passive: a small object on the desk that notices on its own when your attention slips. We scored five concepts on cost, feasibility, effectiveness and how engaging they would be to use, and the desk robot came out on top.

How it decides you’re distracted

  1. Find the eyes. MediaPipe finds 468 points on your face. Six points around each eye are used.
  2. Measure them. The height of each eye is divided by its width. Above 0.20, your eyes are open and on the screen; below it, you’re looking away or down.
  3. Wait before judging. After about 3 seconds of focus the work timer starts counting. A short glance away doesn’t count; a couple of seconds does.
  4. Show it. The transparent screen shows a pair of animated eyes that follow you, the work timer and status messages.
  5. React. The light turns red, the buzzer sounds and the screen shows “DISTRACTED”. After 10 seconds away, the timer resets.
Focused
Timer counts up, light is green
Distracted
Red light, buzzer, a beep every 3 seconds
Away over 10 s
Timer resets to 00:00

Electronics and wiring

Two boards split the work. The Raspberry Pi Zero 2W does the seeing and deciding. The Raspberry Pi Pico runs the lights and sound, so the alerts stay instant even while the Pi is busy with the camera.

The enclosure

The enclosure was modelled in SolidWorks and 3D printed. It took five iterations to fit the breadboard, both boards, the wiring, the LED and the USB port. The first versions were a long, shallow tray; the final one is a compact box with a sliding lid and a camera mount that rotates on a pin, so the camera angle can be adjusted to the person sitting at the desk.

Problems we solved

The camera wasn’t detected

The Pi didn’t see the camera at all. Changing the configuration revealed an EREMOTEIO error, which pointed at the connection, and reseating the ribbon cable the right way round fixed it.

Not enough memory

The Pi Zero 2W has very little memory, so we kept the script lightweight. We started with Haar cascades because they are cheap, but after optimising, MediaPipe ran just as fast and was more accurate, so we switched.

Fitting everything in

Five print iterations to fit the breadboard, both boards, the wiring, the LED and the USB port. The lesson: measure every part properly before the first print.

Faulty parts

The LED and buzzer kept failing. We traced it to faulty components and rewired the feedback circuit four times before it was reliable.

What I’d do next

  • Agree on mockups as a team and take every measurement before the first CAD model.
  • Keep focus statistics and send a monthly summary of your attention habits.
  • A companion app for custom alerts, and a button or voice control on the device.

Built with

Raspberry Pi Zero 2W, Raspberry Pi Pico, Pi Camera, Waveshare 1.51″ transparent OLED, Python with OpenCV and MediaPipe, MicroPython, SolidWorks and 3D printing.
