Autonomous car 2025

A small robot car that finds its own way through a maze. Three ultrasonic sensors measure the walls ahead and to each side, and a Raspberry Pi Pico decides when to drive, correct and turn.

Built for Cornerstone of Engineering I at NU London with one teammate. I built the car, wrote all of its code and designed the sensor holder.

Three challenges

1. A straight line
Drive straight for two seconds. Harder than it sounds: the two motors aren’t identical, so the car drifts.
2. A pre-programmed maze
A fixed route of timed straights and turns. We measured every leg of the maze with rulers and turned the distances into run times.
3. A maze it navigates itself
No route given. The car reads its sensors and decides at every step where to go.

How it finds its way

  1. Measure. Each sensor sends a pulse of sound and times the echo. Distance is the time multiplied by the speed of sound, halved for the round trip.
  2. Wall ahead? Closer than 6 cm, the car stops and turns 90° toward whichever side has more room, as long as that side has at least 10 cm.
  3. Drifting? Within 4 cm of a side wall, it nudges away with a short correction.
  4. Clear? Otherwise it drives forward, checking again every 50 milliseconds.
  5. Out. When both sides are over a metre away, it has left the maze and stops.

Problems we solved

It wouldn’t drive straight

With the same power, one motor ran faster than the other and the car curved. Giving the slower motor about 4% more power evened them out.

Turns weren’t equal

Turns are timed, not measured, and turning one way took longer than the other. We calibrated each direction separately: 0.535 seconds for one and 0.517 for the other to make a clean 90°.

Lost echoes

When a sensor faces open space, the echo never comes back and the reading would hang. A 30-millisecond timeout treats no echo as “far away”, which is also how the car knows it has left the maze.

Timing a route by hand

For the pre-programmed maze, every leg was measured with rulers and converted into run times, then tuned run by run until the car stayed off the walls.

Sensor holder

A 3D-printed bracket holds the ultrasonic sensors at the front of the chassis, with a hole for the screw that fixes it to the car.

Built with

Raspberry Pi Pico, MicroPython, three HC-SR04-style ultrasonic sensors, an L298N-style motor driver with PWM speed control, two DC gear motors, a battery pack and an acrylic chassis.
